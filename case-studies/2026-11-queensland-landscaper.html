A Queensland landscaper, two denials, and a name that finally matched

GBP Case Study Review · November 2026

Published 2 November 2026 · A review, not our case · Source: Search Scope, written by Dorian Menard

We did not do this work. The timeline and documents below are as Search Scope published them. A published recovery is not a promise that the next profile will be restored. Google decides.

This month’s review is a reinstatement, not a ranking sprint. A landscaping contractor in south-east Queensland lost the profile customers use to call, get directions, and confirm the business is real. Search Scope wrote the case up in August 2025 and updated it in May 2026. It is worth reading in full, and it is worth praising for a reason that is easy to skip: they stopped treating the denial as a wording problem and treated it as a records problem.

What they say went wrong

The published account says the trading name and the legal entity name did not line up on the Australian Securities and Investments Commission register and the Australian Business Register. To a reviewer, that can look like two businesses. The suspension had already sat for a couple of weeks before the firm was brought in. That gap matters. Every extra week is a week of calls that never arrive, and it is also a week in which the file gets colder.

Credit to the owner for eventually putting the records on the table, and credit to Search Scope for saying the quiet part in public. A lot of reinstatement write-ups jump straight to “we appealed and it came back.” This one names the mismatch. That is the useful part.

Two denials, then a cleaner file

Their write-up walks through more than one no. An early appeal went in after an audit and was denied. A second appeal followed, this time with a statutory declaration, accountant-certified copies of tax and company documents, and an updated company-register entry. That was denied too, and the case picked up a redress note. Only after that did an escalation, with the evidence pack and a short case summary, bring the profile back. They date the reinstatement as 13 August. The whole path, from the first appeal to the profile staying live, ran just under three weeks, and it included a re-suspension that had to be answered with the same documents again.

That is not a failure of effort. It is what a hard file looks like. The first pass through Google’s appeals tool is often a narrow check. A name that does not match the government record will not become true because the appeal letter is warmer. Search Scope’s better move was upstream of the letter: renew and align the business name, gather utility bills and invoices, and have an accountant certify the copies. The appeal then described a business that the registers also described.

What deserves kudos

Three choices in this case are worth copying, whoever does the next one.

  • They fixed the record, not just the profile. The website, the profile, and the government name were brought into the same sentence. A profile cannot outrun the register it is checked against.
  • They did not answer a denial by repeating the same file. The second submission added a statutory declaration and certified copies. The escalation added a case summary. Each step had a new fact, not a louder tone.
  • They said what did not come back easily. The published note is honest that not every review returns with the pin, and that most of their other reinstatements close faster than this one. A case study that admits the exception is more useful than one that only prints the win.

Search Scope also reports a wider book of work: 295 of 300 suspended Australian profiles reinstated between 1 February 2025 and 31 July 2026, with appeals they aim to file within 24 hours and restorations they describe as often landing 24 to 72 hours after submission. Those are their figures, on their caseload. The five they say they could not recover traced, in their account, to review manipulation or an account-level trust problem. That caveat belongs in the review. Some files are not an evidence problem. No appeal repairs a business that is not eligible, or a record that is not true.

What a careful reader should not take from it

Do not take “under three weeks” as a timetable you can buy. This file had already waited before intake, then absorbed two denials and a re-suspension. Another landscaper, in another country, with a different mismatch, can take longer or can be refused. Do not take a high rate on someone else’s book as a rate for yours. And do not treat an escalation as a shortcut around Google’s guidelines. The win here followed a name the registers would recognize.

If your own profile is down, the first question is still the one this case answered late: does every public record say the same business name, at the same place, for a business that is actually operating? If it does not, the next appeal is early.
